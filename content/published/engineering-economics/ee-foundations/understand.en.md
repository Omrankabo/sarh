## From the situation to a defensible decision

Imagine choosing a pump for a village. A machine must work physically, serve people and use scarce resources wisely. Learn what the economic question is before reaching for a factor.

### 1. One engineering problem lives in two environments

A bridge that cannot carry its load fails physically. A bridge that costs more than the community can support may fail economically even if it is strong. Start with the need, the constraints and feasible alternatives. Microeconomics follows a household, firm or market; macroeconomics studies aggregate output, employment and prices. The lecture also distinguishes the specialist, the coordinator and the manager: each connects technical knowledge to decisions at a different scale.

```text
feasible alternatives → common service → economic comparison
```

**What each symbol means** — An alternative is a technically possible way to deliver the required service. Currency totals must refer to the same scope.

**Try this situation** — Two feasible designs cost 12000 and 15000 CU now and deliver identical service.

**Follow the calculation** — The initial saving is 15000−12000=3000 CU; this alone is a decision only if later costs and outcomes are also equal.

**What the result changes** — A useful economic decision starts with feasible service, not a list of prices.

**When this model applies** — Safety and service requirements are constraints; a cheap unsafe option is not a feasible alternative.

### 2. Physical efficiency is not economic efficiency

Picture fuel entering a power station and useful electricity leaving it. Physical efficiency compares compatible energy quantities. Economic efficiency compares monetary worth with the monetary cost of all inputs included in the model. A thermal station can have an energy efficiency below one while creating economic worth above its cost. Conversely, a technically efficient device can be uneconomic if its input is expensive or its output has little value.

```text
ηph = useful output / physical input; ηec = worth / cost
```

**What each symbol means** — Both efficiencies are dimensionless. For one input and one output: ηec=ηph×(output price per energy unit)/(input cost per energy unit).

**Try this situation** — Electricity sells for seven times the fuel price per energy unit. Fuel is 50% of total cost. Require ηec=1.2.

**Follow the calculation** — ηec = ηph×7×0.5. Therefore ηph=1.2/3.5=0.342857, or 34.286%.

**What the result changes** — Convert physical quantities into their economic values before judging worth/cost.

**When this model applies** — Use the same energy unit and include the stated cost scope; fuel-only cost is not total plant cost.

### 3. Name the cost before using it

First cost starts a project. Operating cost keeps it working. Fixed operating costs stay approximately unchanged over a relevant activity range; variable costs respond to production. Incremental cost asks what changes when the output changes. A sunk cost is already irrecoverable. An old invoice is sunk, but the resale value of a machine you could sell today is an opportunity cost and remains relevant.

```text
C(Q)=F+vQ; average incremental cost=ΔC/ΔQ
```

**What each symbol means** — F is cost per analysis period; v is cost/unit; Q is units per same period. ΔC/ΔQ has units CU/unit.

**Try this situation** — Fixed annual cost is 10000 CU and variable cost 10 CU/unit. Make 500 units.

**Follow the calculation** — C=10000+10×500=15000 CU/year. Another 100 units adds 1000 CU, not another 10000.

**What the result changes** — Past expenditure cannot be changed; a sale forgone today can.

**When this model applies** — The linear model is a local approximation; a fixed cost may change when capacity is expanded.

### 4. Utility, value, price and an exchange

Water may be essential while its price is low; a rare ornamental object can carry a high price. Utility describes usefulness to a person, value expresses an assessment of worth, and price is the monetary exchange amount. Consumer goods satisfy wants directly; producer goods help make other goods. Necessities and luxuries depend on context. The lecture uses exchange to connect two parties who value what they receive more than what they give. Keep those viewpoints separate.

```text
net private benefit = perceived worth − price
```

**What each symbol means** — Worth and price must use a common monetary scale if they are subtracted. Utility need not itself be measured in money.

**Try this situation** — A maintenance tool costs 300 CU and is expected to save 450 CU of immediate work.

**Follow the calculation** — Estimated net immediate benefit=450−300=150 CU; check whether the saving is achievable.

**What the result changes** — Classify by the service a good provides, not its price tag.

**When this model applies** — A subjective benefit estimate is not a market quote or proof of a universal value.

### 5. A worker story: include the machine’s time

A worker produces 10 pieces each hour while using a machine that also costs money per hour. Paying a slower worker in direct proportion to output seems fair to the payroll calculation, but it spreads the same machine-hour cost across fewer pieces. Compare cost per finished piece. This is the lecture’s reason for insisting on the whole economy of a problem: design, materials, personnel and resource inputs interact. A saving in one column can create a larger loss in another.

```text
unit cost=(wage/hour + machine cost/hour)/(pieces/hour)
```

**What each symbol means** — Wage and machine rate are CU/h; dividing by pieces/h gives CU/piece.

**Try this situation** — Worker 1: wage 6 CU/h, machine 6 CU/h, output 10/h. Worker 2 produces 9/h. Preserve unit profit.

**Follow the calculation** — Old unit cost=(6+6)/10=1.2. Set (w2+6)/9=1.2, giving w2=4.8 CU/h. At 5.4/h the unit cost is 1.26667.

**What the result changes** — Preserve the full unit-cost balance, including equipment time.

**When this model applies** — Same machine rate, selling price and other unit costs; no change in quality or rejects.
