## From the situation to a defensible decision

Supplementary practice from the supplied FE/PE materials. Reuse the course’s cash-flow logic for inflation, benefit/cost, replacement and a stated tax model; no guaranteed exam coverage is claimed.

### 1. Inflation changes the measuring ruler

An actual future currency amount includes general price change. A real amount is expressed in a chosen base-year purchasing-power unit. Either inflate the flows and discount at a compatible nominal rate, or keep real flows and use a real rate. Mixing real amounts with nominal discounting can count the effect of inflation twice. Price escalation specific to materials is not necessarily the same as general inflation.

```text
1+inom=(1+ireal)(1+f); ireal=(inom−f)/(1+f)
```

**What each symbol means** — f is inflation per period; nominal and real rates use that same period.

**Try this situation** — Nominal rate 10%, inflation 4%.

**Follow the calculation** — ireal=1.10/1.04−1≈5.769231%, not exactly 6%.

**What the result changes** — Keep the purchasing-power ruler consistent on both sides.

**When this model applies** — Hypothetical given rates; consistent purchasing-power basis and no claim about current market inflation.

### 2. Benefit/cost needs a definition and a common basis

A public project can be evaluated using discounted benefits and costs. Include disbenefits in the way the selected convention specifies, and do not count them both as negative benefits and extra costs. For mutually exclusive scale choices, a larger standalone B/C ratio is not automatically a better use of the available resources. Test the extra benefit against the extra cost under a common horizon and discount rate.

```text
B/C=PWbenefits/PWcosts; incremental ratio=ΔB/ΔC
```

**What each symbol means** — Benefit and cost magnitudes use the same date, horizon and discount assumptions.

**Try this situation** — A has benefits 150 and costs 100; B has benefits 240 and costs 180, in present units.

**Follow the calculation** — B/CA=1.5 and B/CB=1.3333; increment=(240−150)/(180−100)=1.125. The larger option can justify its extra cost despite a smaller standalone ratio.

**What the result changes** — A complete model includes each consequence once, with a clear sign and category.

**When this model applies** — Costs positive in the denominator, a stated disbenefit convention and relevant feasible alternatives.

### 3. Replacement starts from today’s opportunity value

The old machine’s historical price cannot be changed. Its market sale value today can be realized if replaced and is therefore a relevant opportunity cost of keeping it. Compare defender and challenger costs over a common service requirement. Book value matters only through separately specified accounting or tax consequences. For an economic-life search, compute equivalent annual cost for each candidate retention life including its changing salvage and operating expenses.

```text
EUACn=[P−Sn(P/F,i,n)+PW(O&M)](A/P,i,n)
```

**What each symbol means** — For the defender, P is current opportunity value in a keep-from-today analysis.

**Try this situation** — Old purchase 50000, current sale 8000, expected sale in 1 year 6000; annual O&M1000, i=10%.

**Follow the calculation** — One-year keep cost at year 1=8000(1.1)−6000+1000=3800.

**What the result changes** — Book allocation and market opportunity are different measurements.

**When this model applies** — Same required service and stated future costs; do not carry sunk purchase invoices into the comparison.

### 4. A stated tax model connects depreciation to cash

In a simplified exam tax model, taxable operating income is revenue minus cash expense minus depreciation. Tax is that amount times a stated tax fraction, and after-tax operating cash adds depreciation back because it was not a cash expense. A MACRS-style problem should supply the recovery class, convention and percentage schedule; apply each percentage to the stated basis. The older PE booklet is not evidence of today’s tax law.

```text
TI=R−E−D; tax=τ TI; OCF=(R−E)(1−τ)+τD
```

**What each symbol means** — R revenue, E cash operating expense, D depreciation and τ a hypothetical stated tax fraction.

**Try this situation** — R=10000,E=4000,D=2000,τ=25%.

**Follow the calculation** — TI=4000; tax 1000; OCF=10000−4000−1000=5000. Under a supplied 32% schedule on 10000 basis, depreciation is 3200.

**What the result changes** — Learning the arithmetic is distinct from verifying current tax rules.

**When this model applies** — Simplified positive-taxable-income model; losses, disposal tax and jurisdiction-specific rules need separate stated assumptions.

### 5. Sensitivity can reverse a cost ranking

Compare alternatives at several defensible hurdle rates when timing differs. Even cost-only rankings can reverse with rate; the supplied older PE introduction overstates rate independence. A delayed cost is discounted more strongly when the rate rises. Record the rate, service assumption and switching point. Do not label an alternative “best at every rate” because it won at one trial rate.

```text
crossover solves PWA(i)=PWB(i)
```

**What each symbol means** — Use signed worth or positive cost magnitudes consistently; compare the same service.

**Try this situation** — Equal-service costs: A=1000 now; B=1500 at year 5.

**Follow the calculation** — At 0%, A costs 1000 versus 1500. At 10%, B’s present cost=1500/1.1^5≈931.38, now less than A.

**What the result changes** — Independent equivalence checks take precedence over an unchecked generalization.

**When this model applies** — Cash timing can affect ranking; safety and service constraints still apply at every rate.
