## From the situation to a defensible decision

A higher price can reduce the units sold. A larger crew can finish sooner but cost more. Learn to read those two stories with slopes, ratios and a clear definition of the comparison.

### 1. Demand and supply tell different stories

Demand asks how much buyers want at a price, holding other influences fixed. Supply asks how much producers offer. Their intersection identifies equilibrium within the stated model. A change of the good’s own price moves along a curve; a change of income, technology or an input price may shift a curve. Economic relationships depend on behavior and assumptions; do not treat a sketch as an unchanging physical law.

```text
Qd=a−bP; Qs=c+dP; equilibrium: Qd=Qs
```

**What each symbol means** — Q is units/period; P is CU/unit; b,d are positive slope coefficients in these linear examples.

**Try this situation** — Qd=100−2P and Qs=10+P. Find the intersection.

**Follow the calculation** — 100−2P=10+P → P=30 CU/unit; Q=40 units/period. Substitute into both curves.

**What the result changes** — Interpret a market diagram through quantities at the same price, not curve names alone.

**When this model applies** — Local linear curves with other demand/supply influences held constant.

### 2. Elasticity needs a declared percentage base

Elasticity compares percentage quantity change to percentage price change, so it is not simply the slope ΔQ/ΔP. The lecture’s finite-change example uses the larger quantity and larger price as percentage bases. Other courses may use an initial-value or midpoint convention. These conventions can give different finite-change numbers. State the convention first and never silently replace the exam’s stated base. The magnitude is normally used for classifying demand. At the extremes, zero elasticity is perfectly inelastic demand (a vertical curve); infinite elasticity is the perfectly elastic limiting case (a horizontal curve).

```text
|Ed|=(|ΔQ|/Qbase)/(|ΔP|/Pbase)
```

**What each symbol means** — Ed is dimensionless. Elastic >1; inelastic <1; unit magnitude=1.

**Try this situation** — Price changes 30→60 CU; demand 16000→13000. Use Qbase=16000 and Pbase=60.

**Follow the calculation** — |Ed|=(3000/16000)/(30/60)=0.375. Revenue changes 480000→780000 CU/day.

**What the result changes** — Revenue P×Q verifies the financial consequence; it does not remove the need to define percentage bases.

**When this model applies** — Explicitly choose initial, larger-value or midpoint bases. This step’s worked example uses the lecture’s larger-value convention.

### 3. From an elasticity assumption to a price decision

An exam may hold the initial price and demand fixed while testing several new prices. With an initial-base finite-change assumption, each scenario is compared with the original point, not chained to the previous scenario. Multiply the resulting demand by margin per unit to obtain total profit. Constant point elasticity, in contrast, leads to a power curve Q∝P^(−E); it is a different assumption. Keep the stated exam convention visible. The supplied older solutions also use the larger-price base for a price rise, giving Q=Q0[1−E(P−P0)/P]. Fixed initial conditions do not by themselves settle this denominator convention; declare it. At P=300 in the 250/50000/E=2.8 case, this convention gives 26666.666667 units and 1600000 CU contribution rather than 22000 and 1320000 under the initial-price-base rule.

```text
initial-price base: Q=Q0[1−E(P−P0)/P0]; lecture larger-price base for a rise: Q=Q0[1−E(P−P0)/P]; Π=(P−c)Q
```

**What each symbol means** — P0,Q0 are the fixed base point; E is the assumed initial-base finite-change elasticity; c is variable unit cost.

**Try this situation** — P0=250, Q0=50000, E=2.8. At P=300 and c=240, find demand and gross contribution.

**Follow the calculation** — Q=50000[1−2.8(50/250)]=22000; Π=(300−240)22000=1320000 CU before fixed costs.

**What the result changes** — Maximize the product of margin and quantity, within a valid demand range.

**When this model applies** — Use this linear scenario rule only when the problem explicitly fixes that initial-base convention. Require Q≥0.

### 4. A faster crew can be a more expensive crew

Keep one job fixed while adding workers. Elapsed hours may fall, but paid worker-hours are N×H(N). In the lecture’s table, four workers take 15 hours while five take 13. The fifth worker saves two hours of elapsed time but raises total paid hours from 60 to 65. Diminishing returns concerns the changing productivity of an added input; the minimum-cost crew and the shortest-time crew answer different questions. A deadline can change the recommended choice.

```text
labor cost=w×N×H(N)
```

**What each symbol means** — w is CU/(worker·hour), N is workers, H is elapsed hours/job.

**Try this situation** — Compare 4 workers×15 h and 5 workers×13 h, at 4 CU/(worker·h).

**Follow the calculation** — Costs are 240 and 260 CU. Four workers are cheaper; five finish two hours earlier.

**What the result changes** — Optimize only among alternatives that meet all required constraints.

**When this model applies** — Constant wage, comparable quality and no other crew-size costs. Additional equipment and delay costs require extra terms.

### 5. Break-even is a boundary, not a promise

A small workshop pays rent even before making its first unit. Every sale contributes price minus variable unit cost toward that fixed bill. Break-even occurs when the contribution exactly covers fixed cost. If price does not exceed variable cost, selling additional units cannot cover a positive fixed cost in this linear model. A crossover between two machines is similar algebra but compares their two cost curves rather than revenue and cost.

```text
Q*=F/(p−v); Π=(p−v)Q−F
```

**What each symbol means** — F is fixed cost/period; p,v are CU/unit; Q* is units in that period.

**Try this situation** — F=10000, p=30, v=10.

**Follow the calculation** — Q*=10000/(30−10)=500 units. At 600 units profit=20×600−10000=2000 CU.

**What the result changes** — Compare complete cost curves at the required output, not a single cost component.

**When this model applies** — Constant unit price and variable cost, p>v, sufficient demand and capacity, same analysis period.
